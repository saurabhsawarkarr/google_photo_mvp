# Updated Problem Statement — Progressive Memory-Based Search Refinement

## One-line Problem

> **Finding a specific photo is rarely a one-query problem, but users don't have a clear way to turn the additional clues they remember into the next useful search step.**

---

## The Situation

When users try to find a specific photo, they often start with only part of what they remember.

For example:

> **“Sunflower with a bee sitting on it”**

The search may return a large set of relevant photos.

The user may then realize:

> “I think it was taken at a farm.”
> “My sister was there.”
> “It was during monsoon.”
> “It was a close-up photo.”

These additional memories can help narrow the search, but the user has to figure out **what to search next and how to express it**.

This creates a gap between the user's evolving memory and the search experience.

---

## What Our Research Shows

Our research found that users remember photos through different dimensions, including **location, people, objects/visual details, activities and time**. In our interviews, users experienced situations where combining additional details did not always improve retrieval.

For example:

* Resham wanted a **front-facing Ganpati decoration** but received a similar photo from the wrong angle.
* Pritish found relevant results for **“Prachi Vakhre bill”**, but adding a specific date caused the search to fail.
* Naina successfully retrieved a photo using multiple contextual details such as **clothing + location**, showing that multi-dimensional natural-language search can work in some cases.

This means the problem is **not simply that Google Photos cannot understand natural language**. The issue is that users cannot reliably predict which combination of remembered details will produce useful results. 

---

# The Core Problem

### Users have more memory than their initial search contains.

After seeing the first results, users may remember additional information that could help:

```text
Initial memory
     ↓
"I remember a sunflower with a bee"
     ↓
Search
     ↓
Many / incomplete / close results
     ↓
User remembers more
     ↓
"It was probably at a farm"
     ↓
"It was during monsoon"
     ↓
"My sister was there"
```

The problem is that **the search experience does not consistently turn these newly surfaced memories into clear, actionable refinement steps.**

Instead, the user has to decide:

> **What should I add?**

and

> **How should I phrase it?**

---

# Why This Creates a Dead End

Our discovery research identified **search recovery as a recurring failure point**:

* 54 of 111 analyzed journeys involved a search-recovery failure (**48.6%**)
* 54 of 111 ended in abandonment (**48.6%**)
* Result relevance issues appeared in 46 journeys (**41.4%**) 

These figures describe the analyzed discovery corpus and should not be treated as population estimates.

The important point is:

> **When the first search is not enough, users often have no clear indication of what information would be most useful to add next.**

---

# The Opportunity

Instead of making users restart or invent another query, the search experience can become **progressive and adaptive**.

After the initial search, the system can:

### 1. Show what it understood

> **I understood**
>
> 🌻 Sunflower
> 🐝 Bee

### 2. Keep those clues selected

The user doesn't need to enter them again.

### 3. Recognize that the current results are still broad or insufficient

> **I found 200 photos**

### 4. Ask:

> **What else do you remember?**

And provide additional relevant dimensions:

**Who** · **Where** · **When** · **What happened** · **What did it look like?**

### 5. Let the user progressively narrow the results

```text
200 photos
     ↓
+ Farm
     ↓
42 photos
     ↓
+ Monsoon
     ↓
12 photos
     ↓
+ Sister
     ↓
4 photos
     ↓
Photo found
```

---

# The Product Problem We Are Solving

> **When users search for a specific photo from incomplete or evolving memory, they may have additional clues that could help retrieve it, but they lack a clear and adaptive way to understand what the system already knows and add the next useful clue without restarting the search.**

---

# Target User

Our primary target is the **Power Searcher segment**, which represents approximately **31.5% of the analyzed discovery corpus**. These users actively try multiple searches and use contextual details such as people, places, activities and visual characteristics to retrieve a specific photo. 

The solution is particularly relevant when:

* the user knows the photo exists
* the initial search produces many or close-but-not-exact results
* the user remembers additional contextual or visual clues
* the user is willing to continue searching but doesn't know what to try next

---

# Desired Product Outcome

The primary outcome is:

> **Increase successful retrieval of specific photos from incomplete or evolving memories.**

We can measure this through:

* **Retrieval success rate**
* **Time to retrieve the target photo**
* **Search abandonment after the initial search**
* **Percentage of users who use at least one refinement**
* **Number of search attempts required to retrieve the photo**

---

## The Core Insight

The simplest way to explain the entire problem is:

> **The user remembers more after they see the results, but the search experience doesn't help them turn that new memory into the next useful clue.**

And therefore our solution is:

> **Show what the system already understood, then help the user add the next useful piece of memory to progressively narrow the search.**
